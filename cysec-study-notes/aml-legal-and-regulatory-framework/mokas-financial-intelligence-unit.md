# What is MOKAS and what powers does it have?

Cyprus's financial intelligence unit: how it is made up, what it does with suspicious transaction reports, and the instructions it can give to regulated firms. · By the [ExamPass CY editorial team](../about.md) · Last reviewed 25 September 2026 · 5 min read

Tested in: AML · Ch 3, Advanced · Ch 7, Basic · Ch 8

<a id="short-answer"></a>

## Short answer

MOKAS, the Unit for Combating Money Laundering, is Cyprus's **financial intelligence unit (FIU)**, set up under **Article 54** of Law 188(I)/2007. It is made up of representatives of the **Attorney General**, the **Chief of Police** and the **Director of Customs**, and is headed by the Attorney General's representative. It receives and analyses suspicious transaction reports and can instruct a firm to suspend or not carry out a transaction for up to **7 business days**, renewable to a maximum of **30 business days** in total. It is not a supervisor of firms.

<a id="what-mokas-is"></a>

## What is MOKAS and who staffs it?

MOKAS is the Unit for Combating Money Laundering and Cyprus's financial intelligence unit, the national centre that receives, evaluates and analyses reports of suspected money laundering, related predicate offences and terrorist financing. It was established by Article 54 of the AML/CFT Law. Its posts sit within the Law Office of the Republic, headed by the Attorney General, but in its work it is functionally independent and autonomous.

It is made up of representatives of the Attorney General, the Chief of Police and the Director of the Department of Customs, nominated by those officials. Members are seconded by name for at least three years and are treated as investigators under the Criminal Procedure Law. A representative of the Attorney General heads the Unit.

MOKAS is not a supervisory authority. It does not license or inspect firms; that is the job of supervisors such as CySEC and the Central Bank of Cyprus, described in [Who supervises firms for AML?](aml-supervisory-authorities.md)

Terms used in this note

- **Financial intelligence unit (FIU)** — The national body that receives and analyses suspicious transaction reports and passes the results to the authorities.

- **Suspicious transaction report** — A report an obliged entity sends to MOKAS when it knows, suspects or has reasonable grounds to suspect that funds or a transaction, including an attempted one, are linked to money laundering or terrorist financing.

- **Attorney General** — The head of the Law Office of the Republic of Cyprus, whose representative leads MOKAS.

<a id="what-mokas-does"></a>

## What does MOKAS do with suspicious transaction reports?

Obliged entities send their [suspicious transaction reports](../transaction-monitoring-and-reporting/reporting-suspicious-transactions-to-mokas.md) to MOKAS, not to the police, the Central Bank or CySEC. MOKAS analyses them, both case by case and to spot wider trends, and may ask any obliged entity for further information, even one that has not filed a report. It has timely access to financial, administrative and law enforcement information.

When it considers it appropriate, it passes information to the Police, the Tax Department and the Customs Department, for investigation where there is a reasonable suspicion of an offence, or for intelligence. It cooperates with FIUs abroad and does not need a court order to exchange information it has gathered nationally. In exceptional and urgent cases involving terrorism, or organised crime linked to terrorism, it aims to exchange financial information with the FIUs of other EU member states straight away.

It also investigates to trace criminal proceeds that could be frozen or confiscated, executes requests for legal assistance from abroad, issues directives on how it carries out its work, and, where practicable, tells firms how useful their reports were and what follow-up they received. Each year it prepares and publishes an annual report.

<a id="instructions-to-firms"></a>

## What instructions can MOKAS give a firm?

Where there is a reasonable suspicion that a transaction is linked to money laundering or terrorist financing, MOKAS can instruct an obliged entity to suspend or not carry out the transaction, or to monitor the movements on a bank or other account. The instruction serves the analysis of the suspicious transaction or prepares the ground for a freezing or confiscation order.

An instruction to postpone or not execute a transaction lasts up to 7 business days and can be renewed, but never beyond 30 business days in total. A firm that follows it is not in breach of its contract with the customer.

MOKAS can also obtain information on the beneficial owners of companies and trusts, on business relationships, and on bank account holders, signatories and balances, and it can apply to court for a disclosure order.

<a id="how-to-think"></a>

## How to think about it

Picture MOKAS as the receiving end of every suspicion, not as a regulator. Its people come from three places, the Attorney General, the Police and Customs, and are led by the Attorney General's representative. Its sharpest tool is the 7-day stop on a transaction, renewable up to 30 business days. Its public output is an annual report. Anything about licences, inspections or fines points to a supervisor instead.

<a id="common-mistakes"></a>

## Common mistakes

1. **Calling MOKAS a supervisor.** MOKAS is the FIU; CySEC, the Central Bank and the other bodies in Article 59 supervise firms.
2. **Mixing up Articles 54 and 59.** Article 54 establishes MOKAS; Article 59 names the supervisory authorities.
3. **Counting 30 days for each renewal.** The instruction lasts up to 7 business days, and renewals can never take its total validity beyond 30 business days.
4. **Sending suspicious transaction reports to the police or the regulator.** Reports go to MOKAS, which decides what to pass on.
